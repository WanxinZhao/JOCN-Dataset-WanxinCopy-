\begin{abstract}

% Machine learning (ML)-enabled network intelligence requires diverse and accessible data across heterogeneous network domains, whereas open-source datasets jointly covering optical transport, fibre sensing, and wireless access remain limited. This paper presents an open-source cross-domain dataset framework that combines practical measurements with large-language-model (LLM)-driven multi-agent digital twins (DTs) for automated data generation in optical–wireless networks. We provide practical datasets collected from experimental testbeds, including long-term optical transmission performance data over the UK National Dark Fibre Facility, fibre communication and sensing data over deployed urban links, and multi-access wireless performance data involving 5G, Wi-Fi, and LiFi. To support cross-domain fusion, three semantic-module designs are investigated, with the privacy-aware bottlenecked module achieving the most favourable overall privacy–utility trade-off. Furthermore, the proposed LLM-driven multi-agent DT framework translates data-generation requests into executable simulation workflows and iteratively refined dataset outputs. In a GNPy-based optical QoT use case, it produces 8,160 valid samples from 8,192 executed scenarios. The proposed datasets and generation framework provide an open foundation for ML development in future cross-domain network intelligence.


Machine-learning-enabled network research requires both practical telemetry and controllable synthetic data, but optical transport, fibre sensing, and wireless access data are usually collected and released separately. We present a cross-domain dataset framework with two complementary parts: practical datasets organised through a common telemetry and metadata structure, and generated datasets produced through an LLM-driven multi-agent digital-twin (DT) workflow. ``Cross-domain'' denotes common representation, repository, and task-level fusion across domains; the released practical campaigns were acquired independently and are not contemporaneous end-to-end measurements. We evaluate a constructed optical--wireless interoperability benchmark using a leakage-free chronological split. A three-dimensional privacy-aware wireless representation achieves a binary service-risk Macro-F1 of $0.646\pm0.005$ over ten seeds, compared with $0.646\pm0.004$ for a hand-crafted representation and $0.633\pm0.004$ for raw wireless features. Its strongest observed CPE-attribute attack reaches Macro-F1 $0.238\pm0.011$, compared with $0.276$ on raw telemetry; PCA exposes less CPE information but also reduces utility. For generated data, a GNPy feasibility case contains 8,192 execution records but 6,144 unique configurations. A deterministic script enumerates the same configuration set in 0.0063~s, establishing a non-LLM baseline. An artifact audit further identifies duplicated executions, simulator configuration warnings, and a missing version-compatible environment for independent numerical replay. These results position the LLM workflow as a feasibility demonstration and the semantic module as a measured privacy--utility trade-off rather than a privacy guarantee.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has been widely investigated in telecommunication networks to support intelligent and autonomous network operation. In optical networks, ML has been applied to quality of transmission (QoT) estimation, traffic prediction, anomaly detection, and resource allocation~\cite{villa2023IEEEAccess}. In wireless networks, ML-based methods have also been explored for radio resource management, multi-access optimisation, traffic prediction, mobility management, and service quality assurance~\cite{sun2019IEEE}. These advances indicate a clear trend towards AI-native network intelligence, where data-driven models support monitoring, optimisation, and closed-loop control.

% Despite these advances, the performance and generalisation capability of ML models strongly depend on the availability of representative and high-quality datasets. In practical telecommunication networks, however, obtaining sufficient data remains challenging. Real operational data are often difficult to access because they contain sensitive information related to network topology, device configurations, service conditions, customer traffic, and operator policies. As a result, network operators are generally reluctant to disclose such data. Although several open-source datasets have been released for optical or wireless network research, most existing datasets are still limited to a single network domain, a specific task, or a restricted experimental scenario. This limits their applicability to future intelligent networks, where decisions increasingly depend on joint observations across multiple domains.

However, the effectiveness of ML models strongly depends on the availability of representative and high-quality datasets. In practical telecommunication networks, data collection and sharing remain difficult because operational data may contain sensitive information, including network topology, device configurations, service conditions, customer traffic, and operator policies. Although several open-source datasets~\cite{zhai2024alibaba}~\cite{filer2016microsoft} have been released for optical or wireless network research, most of them focus on a single domain, a specific task, or a limited experimental scenario. This restricts their applicability to future intelligent networks, where decisions increasingly rely on joint observations across multiple domains. The lack of open-source cross-domain datasets is particularly important for converged optical and wireless networks, where wireless access and optical transport domains are tightly coupled to support end-to-end services. Service degradation may arise from wireless channel variations, optical transmission impairments, traffic dynamics, or their combined effects. Therefore, cross-domain datasets are essential for end-to-end service monitoring, fault diagnosis, resource coordination, and AI-assisted orchestration. However, constructing such datasets is challenging due to heterogeneous infrastructures, telemetry interfaces, data formats, time scales, and control mechanisms. Privacy concerns in wireless telemetry and confidentiality issues in optical-domain topology and configuration data further complicate data collection, fusion, and sharing. Synthetic data generation can complement practical measurements by covering diverse network scenarios and rare operating conditions. Digital twins (DTs) provide an effective approach for generating synthetic network data by emulating network behaviour under configurable topologies, traffic patterns, device parameters, and service requirements. However, existing DT-based data generation still relies heavily on manual scenario design, parameter configuration, experiment execution, result analysis, and simulation refinement. This process is time-consuming and difficult to scale, especially for cross-domain networks with large configuration spaces and domain-specific constraints.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Cross-Domain Data Availability and Network Telemetry}
 
With the increasing use of ML in telecommunication networks, data have become a key enabling resource for model training, validation, inference, and updating. In cross-domain optical--wireless networks, practical telemetry is particularly valuable because it captures communication performance, device states, service requests, and environmental variations under real operating conditions. In optical transport and fibre-sensing systems, such data may include bit error rate (BER), optical signal-to-noise ratio (OSNR), Q-factor, optical power, route information, configuration records, device alarms, state-of-polarisation (SOP) evolution, backscatter traces, distributed acoustic sensing signals, and vibration-related features~\cite{he2023isac,zhao2025vibration,zhao2026train}. In wireless access networks, standardised telemetry may include throughput, packet loss, delay, signal strength, cell load, mobility, traffic distribution, and per-device access statistics~\cite{3gpp28552,etsi128554}. The value of these heterogeneous data lies not only in individual measurements, but also in their temporal alignment and shared service context across optical transport, fibre sensing, and wireless access domains.


However, practical cross-domain data are difficult to collect, align, and share. Optical transport, fibre sensing, and wireless access systems rely on different infrastructures, telemetry interfaces, data formats, and time scales. In addition, operational data may expose real network topology, configuration, device states, customer traffic, service conditions, and operator policies, thereby limiting public data sharing. Consequently, only a limited number of open optical-network datasets are available. Representative examples include the Microsoft wide-area optical backbone dataset for operational network-performance analysis~\cite{filer2016microsoft,delezoide2020field}, the Fraunhofer HHI QoT dataset collection for ML-assisted QoT estimation in elastic optical networks~\cite{bergk2022qotdataset}, and the Alibaba disaggregated production-network dataset for field-data-based QoT estimation and parameter optimisation~\cite{zhai2024alibaba,he2023qot}. Recent dataset-oriented efforts have further covered space-division-multiplexing networks, fibre sensing, optical-device modelling, and radio-over-fibre systems~\cite{akbari2025sdmdatasets,li2026dasdataset,yuan2026soadataset,zhao2026mmwavearof}. Nevertheless, most existing datasets remain limited to a single domain, topology, task, or measurement campaign, and rarely provide time-aligned optical-transport, fibre-sensing, and wireless-access observations under a common service context.

Network telemetry provides the basis for scalable data collection~\cite{rfc9232}. Model-driven and streaming telemetry solutions based on NETCONF/YANG, gRPC, gNMI, and publish--subscribe mechanisms have been developed to expose network states and performance measurements~\cite{rfc9232,gnmi2018}. In optical networks, prior studies have demonstrated telemetry services for real-time monitoring in disaggregated optical networks, streaming-telemetry-assisted diagnosis, Kafka-based optical monitoring frameworks, and unified monitoring platforms integrating telemetry collection, time-series storage, configuration databases, and streaming pipelines~\cite{paolucci2018telemetry,tanaka2021diagnosis,sgambelluri2022kafka,shen2025telemetry}. In wireless networks, O-RAN and AI-native RAN architectures also rely on multi-source telemetry to expose radio, traffic, and service context to intelligent controllers and AI/ML applications~\cite{mahmoud2026oranintelligence}. However, most existing telemetry frameworks are designed for specific domains, vendor interfaces, or monitoring tasks. Cross-domain data collection, time alignment, format consistency, repository management, and service-level interpretation across optical transport, fibre sensing, and wireless access domains remain insufficiently addressed.

\subsection{Semantic Data Abstraction for Privacy-Aware Cross-Domain Fusion}

Cross-domain dataset generation requires not only collecting heterogeneous telemetry, but also deciding which information should be shared, stored, and fused. Directly aggregating raw wireless telemetry into a central repository may introduce unnecessary communication and storage overhead while exposing device-specific or service-sensitive information. This issue is particularly critical in multi-access wireless networks, where raw telemetry may reveal device identity, access behaviour, traffic patterns, mobility states, and service conditions~\cite{8792139}. Therefore, practical optical--wireless data fusion requires compact and task-relevant representations that preserve useful information for downstream analysis while reducing the exposure of sensitive raw observations.

Task-oriented semantic communication provides a useful principle for such abstraction by transmitting information relevant to downstream tasks rather than reconstructing all source observations with equal fidelity~\cite{xie2022task,ma2023explainable}. Within telemetry systems, semantic representations can be constructed from expert-designed indicators or learned encoders. In particular, information-bottleneck methods can learn compact latent variables that preserve task-relevant information while discarding irrelevant input details~\cite{alemi2017vib}. Such representations can summarise wireless load, link stability, access diversity, congestion risk, and service degradation, thereby reducing data volume and enabling more efficient fusion with optical transmission and fibre-sensing measurements. However, compact representations are not automatically privacy preserving, because sensitive attributes may still be inferred from the transmitted features. Privacy-aware representation learning therefore requires information bottlenecks, noise injection, adversarial objectives, or representation regularisation to balance downstream utility against sensitive information leakage~\cite{alemi2017vib,edwards2016censor,madras2018fair,wang2024privacysemantic}.

Existing studies on semantic-communication and privacy-aware representation mainly focus on single-domain communication data, end-to-end transmission efficiency, or downstream task accuracy. Limited work has investigated how wireless telemetry should be abstracted for cross-domain optical--wireless dataset generation, or how such representations should be evaluated jointly in terms of cross-domain utility, data reduction, and privacy exposure. This motivates the design of wireless semantic modules that transform raw multi-access telemetry into compact task-relevant representations for privacy-aware fusion with optical transmission and fibre-sensing data.

\subsection{Digital Twins and LLM-Driven Data Generation}

The preceding subsections concern the collection and selective sharing of practical telemetry. Synthetic data address a different limitation: even a well-instrumented testbed cannot economically cover every topology, loading condition, device setting, or rare failure. Synthetic data can therefore complement, rather than replace, independently collected measurements by expanding scenario coverage and providing labels under controllable conditions. Network DTs provide a natural execution tool because they emulate network behaviour under configurable topology, traffic, device, channel, and service conditions~\cite{zhuge2023dt}.

In optical networks, GNPy provides an open and vendor-independent physical-layer modelling framework for emulating optical paths under configurable topology, equipment, channel loading, and transmission-parameter settings~\cite{curri2022gnpy}. Recent studies have used GNPy-based DTs to generate optical-network datasets under normal and degraded conditions, such as amplifier degradation, span-loss variation, and component misconfiguration~\cite{mohamed2025dtfailuredb,mohamed2025mlagentsdt}. In wireless networks, simulators and DT platforms, such as ns-3 and Colosseum, support repeatable packet-level, radio frequency, and protocol-stack emulation~\cite{riley2010ns3,polese2024colosseum}, while generative-AI-enabled wireless DTs have been explored for 6G modelling and scenario augmentation~\cite{tao2024wirelessdt,chai2024genaidt}. These studies demonstrate the value of DTs for controllable data generation. Nevertheless, DT-based dataset generation still requires substantial manual effort to define objectives, configure scenarios, execute simulations, validate results, and refine parameter settings. This manual process becomes costly and difficult to scale in cross-domain optical--wireless environments, where different DT tools rely on different abstractions, interfaces, and configuration formats.

Recent studies have begun to integrate LLMs, AI agents, and DTs to reduce the manual burden of network automation. In optical-network DT research, modelling, telemetry, self-learning, and lifecycle management have been identified as key components for intelligent optical networks~\cite{wang2024dtreview}. GPT-enabled DT assistants have also been demonstrated to interpret user intentions and interact with DT functions for autonomous optical-network management~\cite{zhang2024gptdtassistant}. In addition, DTs have been used to provide updated network states and pre-verify LLM-generated strategies before deployment, with demonstrations covering performance optimisation, protection switching, and recovery after fibre cuts~\cite{song2025llmdt}. Other studies have explored fine-tuned local agents, DT-aided wavelength-loading control, AI-agent integration, and field-trial demonstrations for optical-network lifecycle automation~\cite{sun2025localaiagents,liu2024autodtwave,zhuge2025dtaiagent,liu2026fieldtrial}. These studies demonstrate the potential of LLMs and AI agents to interact with DTs, but their focus remains on network operation and management rather than autonomous dataset generation.

The cited DT assistants and AI-agent studies automate parts of modelling and network operation, but they still rely on explicitly supplied objectives and configuration ranges~\cite{zhang2024gptdtassistant,song2025llmdt,sun2025localaiagents,liu2024autodtwave}. This leaves dataset generation as a distinct workflow-design problem. The present work therefore studies an LLM-driven interface that translates a high-level request into structured tasks, invokes optical and wireless DT engines, analyses generated KPIs, and proposes refinements. Section~\ref{sec:LLM} evaluates this as a feasibility demonstration and includes a deterministic configuration-enumeration baseline; it does not claim that an LLM is necessary for exhaustive enumeration.

\subsection{Proposed Cross-Domain Telemetry Architecture}
\label{subsec:4.A}

Figure~\ref{fig:cross-domain telemetry.png} presents the cross-domain telemetry architecture developed from our earlier telemetry platform~\cite{shen2025telemetry}. The implemented elements used for the released practical datasets are device-data acquisition, time-series/configuration storage, and the Kafka transfer path. The semantic-module experiments use exported records from this platform. DT execution is demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:LLM}; the AI-engine and controller blocks are architectural integration points and are not experimentally evaluated here.

\subsubsection{Optical Transport Network Physical Layer}

At the bottom of Fig. \ref{fig:cross-domain telemetry.png}, the physical layer of the optical transport network consists of multiple interconnected optical nodes, including aggregation and intermediate nodes. These nodes represent practical optical transport infrastructure, in which NEs such as transponders, fibre links, optical switches, reconfigurable optical add-drop multiplexers (ROADMs), and optical amplifiers generate heterogeneous operational data.

The collected optical domain data can be categorised into three types: performance monitoring metrics, equipment parameters and topology information, and fibre sensing data. Performance monitoring metrics include receiver-side BER, OSNR, received optical power, amplifier input/output power levels, and per-port optical power measurements from optical switches. Equipment parameters and topology information include transmitter-side launch power, wavelength allocation, modulation format, amplifier gain, node configuration, and link information. Fibre sensing data include SOP, chromatic dispersion (CD), polarization mode dispersion (PMD), and backscatter traces. These data provide communication- and physical-layer visibility, supporting applications such as QoT estimation, anomaly detection, environment-aware operation, and proactive maintenance.

\subsubsection{Distributed Node-Level Databases}
Local databases store node- or domain-specific telemetry. In the implemented platform, continuously sampled BER, OSNR, optical-power, and sensing streams are stored in InfluxDB, whereas topology and equipment configurations are stored in PostgreSQL. Forecasting and anomaly-detection modules are possible consumers, but their deployment and scaling are outside the evaluation in this paper.

\subsubsection{Telemetry Service Design}

The implemented telemetry service ingests available NETCONF, gRPC/gNMI, REST/HTTP, and vendor-interface records through Telegraf or source-specific collectors and writes timestamped values to the local databases.


For repository transfer, selected local records are published to Kafka topics and consumed by the central repository. Campaign and source identifiers are retained during aggregation so that independently acquired records are not mistaken for naturally aligned observations.

% This design decouples local data collection from centralised data consumption. Distributed databases act as domain-level or node-level data producers, while the centralised repository serves as the integration point for cross-domain data fusion. Upper-layer applications do not directly retrieve data from every distributed database; instead, they access the aggregated and aligned data from the centralised repository. This improves system scalability, simplifies application development, and provides a consistent data interface for network-wide analytics and control.

Kafka is a standard implementation component rather than a contribution. Because no throughput or latency benchmark is reported, this deployment is not used as evidence of scalability.

Before wireless data are shared with the central repository, the evaluated semantic modules transform raw telemetry into compact task-related representations. The experiment measures payload dimension, downstream utility, and attribute-inference risk; it does not provide a cryptographic privacy guarantee. The design and evaluation are detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:SemanticModules}.

\subsubsection{Centralised Cross-Domain Data Repository}

The central repository provides a common access point for selected monitoring streams, topology, equipment, and service metadata. Importantly, a common repository does not imply that all released campaigns are time aligned: timestamps and campaign identifiers are retained so users can distinguish contemporaneous records from independently acquired datasets.

\subsection{Semantic Modules for Cross-Domain Data Fusion}
\label{subsec:SemanticModules}

The semantic module is evaluated as an interface for converting wireless telemetry into compact representations. We compare V1, a hand-crafted representation; V2, a learned eight-dimensional latent representation; and V3, a learned three-dimensional bottleneck with optional training-time noise. The term privacy-aware refers to an evaluated design objective---dimensionality and noise are varied and attribute leakage is measured---rather than a formal privacy guarantee.

%Fig.\ref{fig:telemetry service design.png} details the novel design of both the local telemetry service from the physical layer of the optical network to distributed databases and the remote telemetry service from distributed databases to the centralized database in the aforementioned proposed unified platform. 

\subsubsection{Semantic Module Designs}

The input contains 12 features derived from 30-s wireless records: aggregate 5G, Wi-Fi, and LiFi traffic; total traffic; mean Wi-Fi and LiFi signal levels; mean Wi-Fi inactivity; a five-record rolling traffic fluctuation; three RAT-activity indicators; and a combined signal-quality score. RAT activity is defined relative to the 25th percentile of training traffic. All min--max and standard scalers are fitted on the training period only. Four task-oriented targets are then formed: load $L$, access diversity $D$, link stability $S$, and congestion risk $C$. With $q$ denoting combined signal quality, $f$ normalised traffic fluctuation, and $i$ normalised inactivity,
\begin{align}
D &= (a_{5G}+a_{WiFi}+a_{LiFi})/3,\\
S &= \left[0.5q+0.3(1-f)+0.2(1-i)\right]_{[0,1]},\\
C &= \sigma(2L+1.5i+f-1.5S-0.8D),
\end{align}
where $[\cdot]_{[0,1]}$ denotes clipping and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function. These variables were selected to cover traffic intensity, temporal stability, multi-access availability, and a composite risk indicator using fields present across the wireless testbed exports. A confidence value $1-f$ accompanies each transmitted representation. Fig.~\ref{fig:semantic modules.png} summarises the three designs.

V1 transmits eight deterministic indicators: $L$, $S$, $D$, $C$, rolling-mean traffic, traffic fluctuation, signal quality, and inactivity risk. V2 uses a 12--64--32--8 encoder and a symmetric decoder to reconstruct $L$, $S$, $D$, and $C$. V3 uses a 12--32--16--3 encoder and a symmetric decoder. Its primary setting injects zero-mean Gaussian noise with $\sigma=0.05$ at the encoder input during training. The encoder--decoders use mini-batch Adam, batch size 32, at most 200 epochs, and early stopping with patience 12 on validation loss. For downstream utility, the four decoded targets plus confidence are fused with optical features; the privacy attacker receives the latent code plus confidence. The source, exact seeds, preprocessing parameters, and per-seed outputs are released with the experiment scripts.

The optical and wireless campaigns do not overlap in time. We therefore construct an explicitly labelled interoperability benchmark by mapping the ordered 20,960 optical records to the ordered 2,849 wireless records at uniformly spaced relative positions; no timestamp equality is asserted. This benchmark tests schema compatibility and representation utility, not contemporaneous end-to-end behaviour. After one-step target shifting, 2,848 samples remain. Optical risk is defined using the training-period 15th percentile of Q-factor or 85th percentile of BER. Wireless risk is defined as $C>0.65$, $S<0.35$, or $D<0.34$. Binary service risk is their logical OR; four degradation classes denote neither, wireless only, optical only, and both. These are rule-derived benchmark labels rather than field-observed service failures.


\subsubsection{Testing Results}

Samples are partitioned chronologically into 60\% training, 20\% validation, and 20\% test segments, with a ten-record purge gap before each boundary to prevent five-record rolling features crossing partitions. This yields 1,698/560/570 evaluated samples. Every threshold, scaler, encoder, and downstream classifier is fitted after the split using training data only. Utility is measured with a 300-tree class-balanced random forest over ten seeds (3, 7, 11, 19, 23, 31, 42, 53, 71, and 97). We report Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, Macro-F1, AUROC, and AUPRC; Table~\ref{tab:semantic_module_results} shows the primary Macro-F1 results and 95\% confidence intervals.

Privacy is operationalised as attribute inference from the transmitted representation. Dominant CPE and dominant RAT are defined by the largest aggregate traffic contribution in each record. Four attackers are evaluated: class-balanced logistic regression, a 300-tree random forest, a two-layer MLP (32 and 16 units), and histogram gradient boosting. Raw telemetry, PCA, random projection, majority, and stratified-random baselines are included. Because the test set contains 559 Wi-Fi and only 11 LiFi records (and no 5G record), RAT accuracy is dominated by the 0.981 majority baseline and is retained only as a diagnostic. CPE Macro-F1 is the primary privacy metric.

\subsubsection{One-Month Performance Monitoring Data from Optical Transport Network over NDFF}


Figure~\ref{fig:setup.png}(a) presents the experimental setup over part of the UK National Dark Fibre Facility (NDFF), including transponders, erbium-doped fibre amplifiers, wavelength-selective switches, and optical switches. Seven equalised 200-GHz-spaced PM-16QAM/QPSK signals were generated by Facebook Voyager, ADVA FSP~3000 TeraFlex, and Ciena transponders. Voyager and ADVA support 16QAM and QPSK, whereas the Ciena device supports QPSK. The signals traversed the 339-km loop from Bristol through Bradley Stoke, Froxfield, Reading, and Power Gate, with carrier frequencies from 193.25 to 194.45~THz. The launch power was fixed at $-5$~dBm as the operating point used for this campaign; no optimality claim is made. A cloud configuration and monitoring database stored the field-trial measurements.

The released dataset contains nearly one month of BER monitoring data collected from 26th December 2023 to 26th January 2024. Raw BER samples recorded every 10~s were resampled to 15-minute intervals, and converted into Q-factor values using $Q = \sqrt{2} * erfcinv(2*BER)$. This preprocessing reduces short-term measurement noise and aligns the sampling interval with that of the Microsoft open-source dataset. The related data processing scripts are also provided on GitHub~\cite{Shen_dataset_2025}. This dataset enables the study of long-term QoT variations in operational optical transport networks by capturing field-induced effects, including environmental changes, device drift, amplifier behaviour, and operational conditions. We also release a complementary dataset from a previous 986-km NDFF field trial~\cite{YangOFC2023} comprising two fibre links and ROADM nodes, where multi-channel Q-factor measurements were collected for end-to-end QoT prediction. Together, the 339-km and 986-km datasets provide representative short- and long-link field-trial scenarios for QoT estimation, short-term performance forecasting and anomaly detection.

\subsubsection{Four-Week Sensing Data from BDFI and M Shed}

Figure~\ref{fig:setup.png} (b) illustrates the experimental setup for long-term fibre sensing based on bidirectional transmission. The experiment was conducted over two deployed urban fibre links in Bristol: a 7.776-km link connecting our lab in Merchant Ventures Building (MVB) to Bristol Digital Futures Institute (BDFI), and an 8-km link connecting MVB to M Shed Museum. Along the MVB-BDFI route, the fibre passes beneath multiple railway tracks near Bristol Temple Meads Station at the 3.2-km point, where it laterally traverses beneath a railway bridge. In contrast, the MVB-M Shed route does not pass beneath railway tracks.

An ADVA FSP 3000 Teraflex transponder generated two 32-GBaud PM-QPSK optical signals, which were launched into the fibre links through optical circulators to enable counter-propagating transmission. The endpoint Circulators were interconnected through the live field fibre, with the forward and backward channels centred at 193.4 THz and 191.3 THz respectively. At each endpoint, the received optical signal was split using an optical coupler: 90\% of the optical power was directed to the coherent receiver, while the remaining 10\% passed through a polariser and was delivered to a polarimeter and a real-time oscilloscope equipped with a photodetector. This configuration enables simultaneous communication monitoring and fibre sensing. The polariser projects the received state of polarisation (RSOP) onto a fixed eigenstate, allowing environmental disturbances along the fibre to be captured through the measured polarisation dynamics. For vibration localisation, a common trigger from an arbitrary waveform generator was used to synchronise both the polarimeter and oscilloscope.

The dataset spans four weeks and is provided in MAT and CSV formats. It contains communication KPIs, including BER, Q-factor, and OSNR, and polarisation telemetry (Stokes parameters $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$). These measurements were continuously collected by the ISIC end-to-end monitoring platform. Because the data originate from deployed urban fibres rather than laboratory spools, they capture environmental and operational variation. The dataset supports studies of SOP evolution, vibration detection and localisation, communication degradation under physical disturbance, ML-based fibre sensing, and communication--sensing integration.


\subsubsection{24-Hour Status and Performance Data from a Wireless Access Network Testbed}

The wireless dataset was collected from a wireless access network testbed originally developed in the 5G-CLARITY project and further extended in the REASON project. These radio signals and telemetry records originate entirely within that wireless testbed; they are not converted from, transported as, or synchronised to signals on either optical field link. The optical interconnection shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:proposed architecture.png} and~\ref{fig:cross-domain telemetry.png} is a system-level architecture, not the signal source for this campaign. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup.png}(c), the testbed integrates 5G New Radio, Wi-Fi~6, and LiFi. Four fixed CPEs were deployed: CPE1--CPE3 were dual-homed to 5G and Wi-Fi, while CPE4 was tri-homed to 5G, Wi-Fi, and LiFi. All CPEs used MPTCP~v1 in fullmesh mode with the default BLEST scheduler. During collection, CPE1--CPE3 generated randomised iPerf traffic towards CPE4.

The dataset provides 24 hours of time-aligned wireless telemetry, covering per-interface CPE traffic, 5G cell metrics, LiFi cell metrics, and Wi-Fi cell metrics. Specifically, it includes per-interface traffic distribution, 5G temperature, throughput and power, LiFi transmit/receive rates and signal power, and Wi-Fi bitrates, inactivity time and average signal levels. This dataset offers a comprehensive view of heterogeneous wireless performance and supports research on AI-driven multi-access optimisation, multi-connectivity control, MPTCP scheduling, and cross-technology coordination in 5G and beyond networks. By capturing the complementary characteristics of 5G, Wi-Fi, and LiFi in a unified testbed, it also provides a basis for wireless DTs, semantic telemetry abstraction, and cross-domain optimisation with optical datasets.

\section{LLM-Driven Multi-Agent Digital Twins for Automated Cross-Domain Data Generation}
\label{sec:LLM}

While the preceding section presents practical cross-domain datasets collected from real optical and wireless testbeds, continuously acquiring sufficient real-world data for diverse or newly emerging network conditions remains challenging. To complement practical telemetry data and support timely ML model training and updating, this section introduces an LLM-driven multi-agent DT framework for automated cross-domain data generation.



\subsection{Proposed LLM-Driven Multi-Agent Digital Twin Framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_LLM} illustrates the demonstrated data-generation workflow. Users specify the dataset objective, network scope, transmitter constraints, channel configurations, and modulation formats in natural language. The orchestrator passes a shared structured task state through planning, scenario expansion, DT execution, analysis, reflection, and reporting stages.

Topology, device configurations, KPI definitions, constraints, and previous execution artifacts form the network knowledge base. The GNPy demonstration reads JSON topology/equipment files and writes scenario-level request, result, and log artifacts. The physical network and database blocks in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_LLM} provide context; they are not exercised as a live closed-control loop in this study.

The DT layer is an interface to execution engines. The reported experiments invoke the physics-based GNPy model and the packet-level ns-3 simulator. Data-driven and decision/optimisation models shown in the architecture are prospective plug-ins and are not evaluated in this study.

The five named ``agents'' are functional LLM-invocation roles within an orchestrated pipeline rather than independently persistent autonomous entities. The Planner produces the structured study specification; Scenario Expansion proposes executable cases; Results Analysis aggregates KPIs and logs; Reflection proposes changes to the search space; and Report formats the retained artifacts. Simulator wrappers and the knowledge-base interface are their tools, while the orchestrator-owned task state is their shared memory. The roles were separated to give each transition an explicit input/output schema and to retain inspectable intermediate artifacts. This modularity is an engineering rationale, not evidence that Planner or Report must be LLM agents; deterministic functions could replace them, and the released evidence does not quantify the communication overhead of role separation. The public release does not expose separate long-term memories or independent subagent schedulers, so we do not claim those capabilities.

The orchestrator receives the user request, invokes all five roles in sequence, calls the DT wrapper, and records intermediate state. The arrows beneath the roles in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_LLM} represent access to this shared orchestration layer, not direct control by only the final roles; its lower-right placement denotes a supporting control plane rather than the start or end of the execution order. Reflection may trigger another scenario-expansion/execution pass. The experiments below establish that this workflow can produce artifacts for two DT engines; they do not establish lower cost, lower latency, or greater correctness than a conventional script.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Reproducibility Scope}

We evaluate the released GNPy artifacts against an independently implemented deterministic enumerator. The expected Cartesian product comprises four destinations, two modulation/spectrum configurations, 256 eight-slot occupancy patterns, and three launch powers, or 6,144 unique configurations. Set precision and recall are computed on these keys, while repeated executions are counted separately. The deterministic program performs configuration enumeration only; it does not replace natural-language intent parsing or GNPy propagation.

The archived public run contains a report and simulator artifacts but does not record the LLM model/version, system and role prompts, sampling settings, number of API calls, tokens, cost, per-call latency, retry trace, or human interventions. The agent/orchestrator source and tool schema are also absent. Consequently, single-LLM, no-reflection, and repeated multi-agent trials cannot be reconstructed without fabricating experimental conditions. We mark these quantities as not reported and limit the present claim to workflow feasibility. Releasing the exact model snapshot, prompts, tool schema, execution traces, and credentials-independent replay harness is required for a controlled agentic evaluation.

\subsubsection{GNPy-Based Network-Level QoT Data Generation}

In this use case, the workflow generates an optical QoT artifact based on the NDFF topology. The request covers paths from Bristol, Voyager transmitters, eight WDM slots, and QPSK and 16QAM configurations. The Planner forms the study specification, and Scenario Expansion constructs the candidate paths, binary channel-occupancy patterns, and launch-power settings for GNPy execution.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:usecase1}(a), four paths, two modulation/spectrum configurations, and $2^8=256$ occupancy patterns produce 2,048 cases per launch power. The archived first iteration contains 2,048 executions at $-5.5$~dBm, not $-5.0$~dBm. The second iteration contains 6,144 executions at $-5.5$, $-5.0$, and $-4.5$~dBm, thereby repeating all first-iteration keys. The 8,192 execution records therefore reduce to 6,144 unique configurations: 6,120 with status \texttt{ok} and 24 all-zero, no-signal configurations. The descriptive artifact trends in Fig.~\ref{fig:usecase1} include lower GSNR with increasing channel loading and route-dependent launch-power sensitivity. For example, the reported Bristol--Bradley Stoke mean decreases from 26.35 to 22.59~dB between one and eight active QPSK channels at $-5.5$~dBm, while Powergate decreases from 9.95 to 5.24~dB.

We performed two correctness checks. First, scanning all 8,160 released result files found an EDFA-below-minimum-gain warning in every file (65,280 occurrences) and an unmet-ROADM-target-power warning in 6,120 files (12,240 occurrences). Thus, \texttt{status=ok} means that a numerical result was returned, not that its equipment configuration was warning-free. Second, 96 cases stratified by path, format, power, and channel load were independently prepared using the released topology and equipment files. With GNPy~2.14.2, all 96 stop at equipment-schema validation because \texttt{target\_extended\_gain=30} and \texttt{max\_loss=0} are outside the accepted ranges and duplicate QPSK/16QAM mode entries are present. A compatibility check with GNPy~2.13.0 gives the same error. The inspected public commit is \texttt{f11545c642976607a87b5dc7b9c91b5b8753e0e9}, but it does not pin the original working GNPy commit or container. We therefore report the published numerical trends as artifact evidence, not as independently reproduced values.

Finally, QPSK and 16QAM are not a single-factor modulation comparison in the released inputs: they use 37.5- and 50-GHz slots, respectively. Across 3,060 otherwise paired valid configurations, the mean absolute GSNR difference is 0.478~dB (maximum 0.808~dB). This difference must be attributed to the joint modulation/spectral-spacing configuration rather than modulation alone.

For the Bristol–Bradley Stoke path, increasing the launch power from (-5.5) to (-4.5) dBm reduces the average GSNR by approximately 1.59 dB. In contrast, the longer paths exhibit considerably smaller variations within the investigated power range; in particular, the Powergate path achieves a slight GSNR improvement as the launch power increases. These results indicate that the influence of launch-power adjustment is route dependent, motivating iterative scenario expansion rather than fixed-parameter dataset generation.

% Figure~\ref{fig:usecase1}(d) further examines the impact of channel loading using QPSK transmission at (-5.5) dBm. For all four NDFF paths, the mean GSNR decreases as the number of active WDM channels increases. Specifically, the mean GSNR of the Bristol–Bradley Stoke path decreases from 26.35 dB with one active channel to 22.59 dB with all eight channels active. Similarly, the corresponding GSNR values decrease from 10.21 to 5.48 dB for Froxfield, from 10.11 to 5.39 dB for Reading, and from 9.95 to 5.24 dB for Powergate. This consistent degradation trend demonstrates that the generated dataset captures channel-loading-dependent performance variation, which is important for QoT prediction and network optimisation under dynamically changing spectrum occupancy.

Overall, the use case demonstrates conversion of a high-level request into structured simulator artifacts and a reflection-triggered second execution round. It does not demonstrate that the LLM is more efficient than deterministic enumeration or that the released numerical values are independently reproducible. Extension to larger channel spaces and other topologies remains future work and would require an explicitly versioned simulator environment and renewed correctness audit.


\subsubsection{ns-3-Based Multi-Access Network Performance Data Generation}

In this use case, the proposed LLM-driven multi-agent DT framework assists in the automatic construction of ns-3 simulation scenarios for synthetic wireless dataset generation. The goal is not to reproduce the practical 5G/Wi-Fi/LiFi testbed described earlier, but to show that the framework can turn a high-level wireless multi-access request into an executable ns-3 simulation. The user requests an indoor scenario with three mobile CPEs connected to three heterogeneous access branches, namely Wi-Fi, an LTE/EPC-based cellular branch, and a LiFi-like access link. The target KPIs include per-flow throughput, packet loss, mean delay, and mean jitter over a 10~s simulation.

Since the stock ns-3 distribution does not provide a native LiFi module, the LiFi-like branch is emulated using standard ns-3 primitives and recorded as a LiFi-surrogate branch. Here, ``LiFi-surrogate'' means a simplified proxy used for simulation, rather than a physical LiFi model. The Planner Agent formulates the request as a wireless multi-access data-generation task, while the Scenario Expansion Agent converts it into an executable ns-3 configuration with a Wi-Fi branch, an LTE/EPC branch, and a CSMA-based LiFi-surrogate branch. Each CPE is modelled as a multi-homed node with separate network interfaces for the three access branches. The ns-3 DT then compiles and executes the generated scratch program, producing per-flow simulation traces and an aggregated KPI file. During this process, the Reflection Agent updates the scenario and code-generation constraints according to build and runtime feedback until valid simulation outputs are obtained.

Together with the preceding GNPy use case, it shows that the same agentic workflow can invoke different DT execution engines and generate domain-specific synthetic datasets while preserving the modelling assumptions required for correct interpretation.

\section{Conclusions}
\begin{comment}
In this study, we presented an open-source cross-domain dataset framework for optical transport and wireless access networks, integrating practical network measurements, semantic data abstraction, and LLM-driven multi-agent DT-based data generation. The released practical datasets include long-term optical transmission performance over the NDFF, fibre-sensing measurements over deployed urban fibre links, and multi-access wireless network performance involving 5G, Wi-Fi, and LiFi. In addition, the proposed privacy-aware semantic module provides compact and task-relevant wireless representations for cross-domain fusion while achieving a favourable privacy–utility trade-off.

To complement practical measurements, the proposed LLM-driven multi-agent DT framework enables automated data generation and iterative refinement under configurable network conditions. In the GNPy-based optical QoT use case, the framework generated 8,160 valid samples from 8,192 executed scenarios, covering multiple NDFF paths, modulation formats, channel-occupancy patterns, and launch-power settings. In the ns-3-based wireless use case, it generated multi-access performance data across Wi-Fi, LTE, and LiFi-surrogate branches, including throughput, packet loss, delay, and jitter KPIs. Overall, the released datasets, together with the proposed telemetry and data-generation framework, provide an open and extensible foundation for ML training, evaluation, and updating in future cross-domain network intelligence. Future work will extend DT-based data generation to more diverse wireless access scenarios and investigate joint learning from practical and synthetic cross-domain datasets.

% Future work will focus on larger-scale optical–wireless co-simulation, more realistic wireless PHY/MAC modelling, and joint learning from practical and generated datasets.

\end{comment}

We presented a dataset framework containing independently acquired practical optical, sensing, and wireless campaigns and demonstrated DT-generated artifacts. Cross-domain in this work means common publication, metadata, representation, and constructed task-level fusion; it does not mean that the practical campaigns are simultaneous end-to-end measurements. On the constructed benchmark, the three-dimensional V3 representation preserves V1-level binary utility and moderately reduces observed CPE-attribute leakage relative to raw telemetry and V1. PCA leaks less but loses more utility, and V3 does not provide a formal privacy guarantee.

The LLM-driven workflow is retained as a feasibility demonstration. Its GNPy artifact has 8,192 execution records but 6,144 unique configurations, all of which a deterministic script enumerates in 0.0063~s. The audit identifies duplicate executions and material simulator warnings, while current GNPy releases cannot load the public equipment file; exact model/API traces and the original simulator environment remain necessary for controlled reproduction. The ns-3 case demonstrates a second DT interface but is not used to claim realistic LiFi modelling. Future work will release version-pinned containers and full agent traces, repair and independently replay the optical configurations, collect genuinely contemporaneous optical--wireless sessions, and evaluate larger scenario spaces with correctness and scalability metrics.
